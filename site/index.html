Electronic payments with the privacy of physical cash.

Source: Wei Dai, *b-money* (1998).

Chapter IV

RPOW

Reusable Proofs of Work · 2004 – 2005

19722014

No notes for this slide.

2004 · Age 48 · He built it

RPOW

Working digital-money software — not merely a proposal.

19722014

Hal Finney released Reusable Proofs of Work—RPOW. Proof-of-work tokens could be exchanged and transferred repeatedly. Hal had built functioning digital-money software, not merely published a proposal.

Source: Hal's August 2004 RPOW announcement. GitHub upload dates are later archival dates.

(Original scene 22)

August 15, 2004 · Cryptography mailing list

An open
invitation.

Hal asked cryptographers to test his Hashcash-based server — and to criticize it.

19722014

Hal invited cryptographers to test his new Hashcash-based server. He released software and documentation, exposing the system to criticism and technical review. RPOW was an experiment he wanted people to challenge.

Source: MetzDowd Cryptography mailing-list archive, August 2004.

(Original scene 23)

August 18, 2004 · The critic

“I’m wondering how applicable RPOW is.”
“…aren’t we restricted to applications already nailed down by digital cash schemes?”
Ray Dillinger (“bear”), Cryptography mailing list
19722014

Ray Dillinger questioned what transferable proof-of-work tokens were actually good for. If work could be transferred and reused, what gave the tokens practical value? It was a difficult question for anyone attempting to invent new money.

Source: Original mailing-list correspondence.

(Original scene 24)

August 19, 2004 · Hal answers

“Another way to think of RPOWs is as an implementation of Nick Szabo’s concept of ‘bit gold’.”
Hal Finney, Cryptography mailing list
19722014

Hal connected RPOW directly to Nick Szabo's Bit Gold. He imagined computationally scarce tokens being bought and sold, if people found a compelling use for them. Four years before Bitcoin, he was wrestling with the economics of digital scarcity.

Source: Original mailing-list response by Hal.

(Original scene 25)

August 21, 2004 · Adam Back replies

Two future Bitcoin
pioneers, debating.

Back saw RPOW as Chaumian e-cash, built from Hashcash and trusted hardware.

19722014

Adam Back recognized the connection between RPOW and Chaumian electronic cash. He understood how Hal was using Hashcash and trusted hardware to prevent unauthorized token creation. Two future Bitcoin pioneers were openly debating transferable proof-of-work money.

Source: MetzDowd August 2004 thread.

(Original scene 26)

The IBM coprocessor

Trust still lived
inside an IBM chip.

Even the operator couldn’t cheat — but everyone depended on trusted hardware.

19722014

RPOW's protection against double spending rested on a special IBM secure coprocessor. Hal tried to make even the server operator unable to cheat. But users still depended on the integrity and continued operation of trusted hardware.

Source: RPOW technical documentation.

(Original scene 27)

The weakness

If the server failed,
the tokens could fail.

One machine’s fate should not decide whether someone’s savings survive.

19722014

If an RPOW server permanently stopped operating, some tokens associated with it could lose their usefulness. For a system hoping to become money, that was a serious weakness. A single machine's fate should not determine whether somebody's savings survive.

Source: Hal, *World of RPOW*.

(Original scene 28)

World of RPOW

A shared
database?

Several RPOW servers keeping one common record of spent tokens.

19722014

Hal considered having multiple RPOW servers maintain a shared record of spent tokens. That required keeping their histories consistent while preventing double spending.

Source: Hal, *World of RPOW*. The rejected architecture was not Bitcoin's later consensus system.

(Original scene 29)

Hal Finney · World of RPOW

“Ultimately, this approach was considered and rejected.”
Hal Finney, RPOW documentation: clustering and shared databases
19722014

He judged the proposed design too complex for RPOW and rejected it.

Source: Hal, *World of RPOW*. The rejected architecture was not Bitcoin's later consensus system.

(Original scene 29)

The unsolved question

How can computers
that don’t trust

who owns the money?

19722014

How can computers that don't trust one another agree about who owns the money? Hal had confronted the problem. He had not published a permissionless consensus solution. This was the missing step between working digital tokens and decentralized money.

Source: Historical synthesis; avoid asserting Hal personally solved this later.

(Original scene 30)

February 2005 · CodeCon

Peer-to-peer

19722014

Hal experimented with linking RPOW to BitTorrent. Uploading and sharing files could earn tokens; spending tokens could purchase priority. He was exploring economic incentives inside peer-to-peer software years before Bitcoin's launch.

Source: Hal's February 2005 announcement.

(Original scene 31)

February 27, 2005 · RPOW 1.2.0

Add Tor.

Proof of work, transferable tokens, peer-to-peer apps — and anonymous communication.

19722014

Hal added Tor support to RPOW, helping users connect with greater network privacy. His experimental money now incorporated proof of work, transferable tokens, peer-to-peer applications, and anonymous communications.

Source: RPOW release log February 27, 2005.

(Original scene 32)

February 2005

19722014

The last verified public RPOW release appeared in February 2005. Hal did not disappear: he continued cryptographic work. But the surviving public RPOW record no longer tells us whether he was pursuing the deeper monetary problem.

Source: RPOW news archive; gaps in public records are not proof of private Bitcoin development.
